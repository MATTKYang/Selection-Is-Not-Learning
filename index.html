Self-evolving language models train on tasks they write for themselves, and every round must decide which updated model the next round inherits. Current loops decide by choosing tasks that look learnable, then inherit every update. We find that most of those updates do not help. HeirCommit judges the updated model itself on fresh questions. EvoHeir goes further and lets experience survive even when the model that produced it is rejected.

Task selection does not predict learning

Pass rate, disagreement, and frontier proximity describe the model before training. They say little about what an optimizer update will do to it. We call the mismatch the selection-to-update gap.

One EvoHeir round

EvoHeir gives exploration two routes into the next learner: the parameters it selects and the experience it absorbs. A useful experience donor no longer has to supply the next model's weights.

1Explore

Train native branches from the current model, the root, exactly as the host loop would.

2Screen

Freeze the candidates and score each against the root on shared screening probes. The best one becomes the recipient.

3Recover

The recipient attempts current and archived problems without references. A frozen copy repairs its failures with a donor's reference and preserves its successes.

4Confirm

HeirCommit scores the final proposal against the root on held-out probes and commits it only if it wins. Otherwise the root carries on.

HeirCommit cuts harmful commits on every host

We took 64 native updates from each of three self-evolution hosts and scored them on a pool kept apart from the decision probes. HeirCommit commits 23–52% of updates and keeps 78–87% of the useful ones.

EvoHeir leads on every backbone

Ten rounds of self-evolution on four backbones, three seeds each. EvoHeir has the highest math and general-reasoning averages on all four and beats Agent0 by 1.59–2.40 pp on the three base models. Of its components, the final confirmation step matters most: removing it costs 2.02 pp.
